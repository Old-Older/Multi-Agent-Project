**US equity = 75% of the portfolio directly (VOO 45% + QQQM 10% + AVUV 10% + stocks 10%) plus ~9% inside VXUS (15% × ~60% US) ≈ 84% of the equity book, or ~74% of the total portfolio.** That is a high but defensible equity weight for a 25-year-old. ### Execution notes
- Convert HKD→USD in **one or two separate conversions** through a low-spread broker (IBKR-type spreads ~0.02–0.03% vs ~0.4–0.6% at some HK retail brokers — on HKD 1m that is roughly HKD 200–300 vs HKD 4,000–6,000). ⚠ Broker spreads are third-party estimates.
- **Stagger entry over 3 months** (e.g. 40% / 30% / 30%) to reduce the risk of buying at a single bad price. This is a behavioural tool, not a return forecast.
- **Rebalance once a year** or when any part of the plan has moved more than 5 percentage points away from its target.
- **Tax point:** US-listed ETFs pay dividends net of **30% US withholding** for HK residents (no US–HK treaty; this applies to **dividends only** — capital gains are not withheld). An Ireland-domiciled UCITS S&P 500 ETF (e.g. CSPX/VUAA) suffers only 15% US withholding on the dividends its underlying US stocks pay, and is not a US-situs asset for estate tax — **already relevant at your starting balance**, not just later. ⚠ Tax figures from secondary sources; confirm with a tax adviser.

## 4. Why this allocation (the reasoning)
1. **Equities are the right asset class for a 10+ year growth goal.** US large caps returned **9.6%/yr nominal over 1928–2022** vs **4.6%** for 10-year Treasuries and **3.3%** for T-bills (Damodaran/NYU Stern). 9.6%/yr is a 1928–2022 average, **not a forecast from today's valuation** — starting from high multiples, the next decade's return is likely to be lower.
2. **Cost is the one thing you control.** The average yearly fund fee across this portfolio is roughly **0.06–0.07%** — about HKD 600–700 a year on HKD 1m. A 1%-fee product would cost ~HKD 10,000 a year and, compounded over 30 years, can consume a fifth or more of the final value.
3. **Diversification across *drivers*, not just tickers.** VOO, QQQM and the stock sleeve all lean on the same mega-cap tech factor (the "Magnificent 7" were ~35% of S&P 500 market cap but only ~22% of its earnings — ⚠ 2025 data). AVUV (small-cap value), VXUS (non-US) and BND (bonds) earn from different sources, which is what actually lowers portfolio risk.
4. **A deliberate growth tilt, sized so it cannot sink you.** QQQ's 10-year 19.2%/yr (QQQ, not QQQM — QQQM is younger) is a period artefact of the 2016–2026 tech boom, not a forecast. 10% keeps the upside optionality while capping the damage if tech de-rates further — the Nasdaq-100 is already ~12% below its October 2026 peak.
5. **Bonds and cash are not there for return.** BND yields ~4.15% and SGOV ~3.62% (both 30-day yields, 21 Nov 2026). Their job is to (a) cut the portfolio's worst drawdown, (b) give you money to buy equities with when they are cheap, and (c) stop you from being forced to sell at the bottom.
6. **The HKD peg makes USD assets cheap to hold.** The Hong Kong dollar is kept between 7.75 and 7.85 per US dollar, so the exchange rate can move by at most about 1.3% while the peg holds — small enough that we do not need to pay for currency hedging. It does not remove currency risk: a change to the peg itself is unlikely but not impossible, and if you eventually spend in RMB, USD/CNY moves are unhedged.

## 7. Plain-language introduction (read this first)
*This is a practice plan with pretend money — nothing has been bought, and it is not personal financial advice.*

You are 25 with HKD 1,000,000 and a long time ahead. The single most reliable way ordinary investors have built wealth is to own a small slice of many good businesses for a long time and pay almost nothing in fees — the yearly charge a fund takes out of your money, which here is about HKD 600–700 a year on HKD 1m. That is what most of this plan does: about 45% goes into one fund that owns the 500 largest US companies, and another 35% spreads into smaller companies (10%), non-US companies (15%) and a fund that leans towards technology companies (10%, which means it will swing more than the rest). Ten percent is in five well-known companies so you can learn how stock picking feels with money you can afford to lose. The last 20% sits in bonds and cash — not to get rich, but to keep you calm and to give you money to buy when prices fall.

The catch is real: in a bad year this portfolio could be worth 30–40% less than you put in — that is, HKD 1,000,000 could show as about HKD 600,000–700,000 on your statement, even though you have not sold anything — and in a 2008-style crash closer to 45% less. That is the price of the higher long-run return, and the plan only works if you don't sell at that moment. Start with the rules in §6, invest in stages, and check it once a year — not once a day.

**If you only do three things:** read §6, invest in three stages rather than all at once, and do not check the balance every day.

**Words used in this document**
- *Forward P/E* — the price of the US market divided by the profits analysts expect companies to make next year; a high number means you are paying more for each dollar of profit.
- *Expense ratio* — the yearly fee a fund charges, shown as a percentage of your money.
- *Drawdown* — how far a portfolio falls from its highest point before it recovers.
- *Rebalancing* — once a year, selling a little of what has grown and buying what has shrunk, to get back to the plan.
- *Withholding tax* — tax the US government takes out of dividends before they reach you — 30% for Hong Kong residents.
- *UCITS* — a European fund structure; an Ireland-based one pays only 15% US dividend tax instead of 30%.
