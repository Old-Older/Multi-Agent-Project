## 1. Introduction — in plain language

You are 25 and have HKD 1,000,000 (about **USD 128,000** at the HKD/USD peg of ~7.80). With a horizon of at least 10 years — realistically 20–30 — the biggest risk you face is not a bad year in the market — it is **staying in cash** and letting inflation erode your savings.

**The plan in one paragraph.** Put **90% of the money into US stock funds** — mostly a broad S&P 500 fund, plus a slice of smaller companies and a small amount outside the US — and keep **10% in short-term US Treasury bills** as a cash cushion. Buy in over 6 months rather than all at once. Then leave it alone, add money when you can, and rebalance once a year. Expect the portfolio to fall 30–40% at some point; that is a normal part of owning shares and is the price of the higher long-run return. In a 2008-style crash the whole portfolio could fall about **45%** (roughly HKD 550,000 left of HKD 1,000,000) — that is a tail event, not the expected bad year, and if it is too much for you we cut shares to 70%. The one thing that would really hurt you is selling in the middle of such a fall.

**Three things to understand before you start:**
1. **Shares go down as well as up.** The S&P 500 fell ~49% in 2000–02, ~57% in 2007–09, ~34% in 2020 and ~25% in 2022. It recovered every time, but the 2000–02 fall took about 7 years to get back to its old high (price basis).
2. **The index is more concentrated than it looks.** The seven largest US companies are roughly **34–39% of the S&P 500** by weight (estimates Aug–Oct 2025) but only ~27% of its earnings (Aug 2025). A "diversified" index fund is therefore partly a bet on big tech and AI.
3. **Taxes and currency matter for a Hong Kong investor.** The US withholds **30%** of dividends paid to HK residents (there is no HK–US tax treaty), and US-listed holdings above **USD 60,000** can be exposed to US estate tax. Both are manageable — see Section 6.

**A worked example in HKD.** If you invest HKD 1,000,000 and the portfolio returns 7% a year after fees, it becomes about HKD 1.97m in 10 years and about HKD 3.87m in 20 years. If it returns 4% a year, the same money becomes about HKD 1.48m and HKD 2.19m. Nobody can promise either number — the point is that **time and the amount you keep adding** matter far more than picking the perfect moment to buy.

**Words used in this document:** *ETF* = a fund that trades like a share; *S&P 500* = an index of 500 large US companies; *forward P/E* = price divided by next year's expected earnings (a rough "how expensive is it" measure); *tranche* = one slice of a purchase made in stages; *sleeve* = one part of the portfolio; *UCITS* = an Irish/EU-regulated fund structure; *W-8BEN* = a US tax form for non-US investors; *US-situs* = assets legally located in the US for tax purposes.

**Split: 90% shares (80% US companies + 10% non-US) and 10% cash-like T-bills.** Everything is USD-denominated.

Notes on the choices:
- **VOO + VTI overlap ~86%** — VTI is used only to add the small/mid-cap tail, not as a second core. Do **not** also add QQQ: it overlaps VOO ~53% and is ~80% tech, which would double the concentration risk in §1.
- **AVUV** is the deliberate tilt away from megacap tech: small-cap value has lagged for a decade, so it is the cheapest part of the US market and the natural counterweight to an S&P 500 core.
- **VXUS** is a valuation hedge, not a return forecast: US large caps compounded ~14.8% a year over the 10 years to 31 Dec 2025 vs ~8.9% for developed ex-US (MSCI EAFE, period unverified). VXUS also holds emerging markets, so the comparison is not like-for-like. Assuming the US gap repeats forever is the biggest assumption risk in a US-only portfolio.
- **SGOV** is not a return engine; it is the buffer that lets you rebalance into a falling market instead of selling.

**Entry plan:** buy the 10% SGOV sleeve up front, then convert HKD→USD and buy the 90% equity sleeve (HKD 900,000) in **6 equal monthly tranches of ~HKD 150,000**. This averages both the exchange rate and the entry price. If the market falls more than 10% during the entry period, bring the remaining tranches forward.

**Rebalancing:** once a year, or when any sleeve drifts more than 5 percentage points from target. Use new money first, then sell.

## 4. Why this allocation — the reasoning

1. **Time is your biggest asset.** Over 20–30 years, shares have historically been the highest-returning mainstream asset class. A 90/10 growth mix suits a 25-year-old with no income need; a bond-heavy portfolio would give away the compounding that matters most at your age.
2. **Core plus satellites, not stock picking.** ~70% sits in broad US index funds (CSPX/VOO + VTI) at very low cost (0.03–0.07% a year, indicative). You get the market's return rather than a manager's guess, and low fees compound: a 1%-a-year fee difference can cost roughly a quarter of your final wealth over 30 years.
3. **Diversify the *kind* of US exposure.** The S&P 500 is concentrated in seven AI/tech names (34–39% of weight, Aug–Oct 2025, vs ~27% of earnings, Aug 2025). Adding small-cap value (AVUV) and ex-US (VXUS) reduces the chance that one theme — AI spending — decides your entire outcome.
4. **Valuations are less stretched than a year ago.** The S&P 500 forward P/E is ~17.7x (Nov 2026, indicative), the lowest since Nov 2023 and below its 5-year average of 19.8x (10-year average 19.1x), while earnings estimates have been rising. A better starting point than 2024–25 — but not a forecast.
5. **Leadership is broadening.** In 2026 small caps and value have outperformed megacap tech, and Mag 7 earnings growth fell below the rest of the index in Q3 2026 (FactSet). A portfolio that owns both sides of that rotation is more robust than one that bets on either. (Small-cap valuation data is unverified.)
6. **Cash is a tool, not a drag.** 10% in T-bills (yield ~4% at the time of writing, unverified and will move with Fed policy) funds rebalancing and unexpected costs without forcing a sale at the bottom.
7. **The peg makes USD investing cheap for you.** The HKD is linked at 7.75–7.85, so USD assets carry only ~1.3% of translation risk — far less than for most non-US investors.

If a 45% fall would make you sell, tell me and we will cut equity to 70% (expected worst case ~35%). Historically a fall of that size recovered in about 5–6 years.

## 6. Practical implementation for a Hong Kong investor

1. **Broker:** a HK broker with US market access. Compare FX spread, commission and custody fees — as illustrative targets, aim for an FX spread under 0.1% and commission of USD 1–5 per trade (these are our targets, not market facts).
2. **Forms:** file a **W-8BEN** — a US tax form that tells the broker you are a non-US investor; without it the broker may withhold more than the standard 30%.
3. **Tax structure choice (important):**
   - *Option A — all US-listed ETFs (VOO/VTI/AVUV/VXUS/SGOV):* lowest fees (0.03–0.25% a year, AVUV figure unverified), best liquidity, but 30% dividend withholding and US estate-tax exposure on ~USD 68,000.
   - *Option B — Irish-domiciled UCITS for the core (e.g. CSPX):* 15% withholding on US dividends and **no US estate-tax exposure** on that sleeve, but slightly higher fees (~0.07%, unverified — check the issuer page) and wider spreads.
   - *Suggested:* Option B — core S&P 500 in CSPX, satellites in US-listed ETFs. Confirm with a HK/US tax adviser; this is general information, not tax advice.
4. **Record-keeping:** keep trade confirmations and a simple spreadsheet of contributions.
5. **Ongoing contributions:** add a fixed amount monthly (even HKD 2,000–5,000). At 25, your savings rate matters more than stock selection.

## 7. What would change this proposal

- You need the money within 5 years (home, relocation) → cut equity to 50–60%.
- A 45% fall is too much → cut equity to 70% and raise T-bills/bonds.
- You later want income → add a dividend ETF (e.g. SCHD), accepting extra withholding drag (yield data unverified).
- The AI spending cycle breaks or US valuations re-rate sharply → rebalance, do not abandon the plan.
- Your tax residency changes → revisit the UCITS vs US-listed choice.
