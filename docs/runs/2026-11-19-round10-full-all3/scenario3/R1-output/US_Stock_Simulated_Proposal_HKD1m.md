# Simulated US-Stock Investment Proposal — HKD 1,000,000, Age 25

**SIMULATED — a practice run. Nothing is bought, no money leaves your account, and no one is charging you for it.** This is a simulated, personalised illustration prepared for discussion. It is not a recommendation to buy or sell, and it is not a substitute for advice from a licensed adviser.

**Limits of this analysis (read before the numbers):** all data are point-in-time **18–19 November 2026** and will move. The Fed's expected policy path could not be sourced and is not stated. 2025/2026 consensus earnings growth is *derived* from the forward-vs-trailing P/E gap, not from a primary consensus provider. ETF expense ratios, AUM and yields come from a third-party aggregator (ETF BFF) — confirm with the issuer before any real trade. Nasdaq-100 drawdowns are price-index and approximate. Portfolio drawdowns are calendar-year based and understate intra-year troughs. Past performance does not predict future returns.

**Client profile (as instructed):** age 25 · HKD 1,000,000 of new money · objective: long-term capital growth · horizon: 10+ years · risk tolerance: can hold through a 30–40% fall · constraints: no leverage, keep ~5% cash, no ESG exclusions requested · all money is new.

## 1. Plain-language introduction (read this first)

This is a practice run: nothing is bought, no money leaves your account, and no one is charging you for it.

You are 25 and you will not need this money for at least ten years. That is your biggest advantage: you have time to recover from bad years.

Here is the whole plan in five sentences.

1. **We put about 90% of your HKD 1,000,000 into US stock ETFs and about 10% into short-term US government IOUs (Treasury bills) and cash.** An ETF is a basket of hundreds of companies that you buy as one unit, like a single stock — you will not own individual shares like Apple or Tesla, you own a basket. Treasury bills are loans to the US government repaid in under three months; they are the closest thing to cash in the market, but they are not bank deposits and are not deposit-protected.
2. **Inside the stock part we spread the money** — about 60% the whole US market, 15% big technology companies, 7% smaller companies, 8% outside the US — so you are not betting everything on one idea.
3. **We buy in stages over about six months** rather than all at once. That is not a prediction that markets will fall; it is to make the first few months easier to live through if they do.
4. **Historically, US stocks have returned about 7–9% a year over long periods — that is history, not a promise, and not something to plan on.** What you should plan on is that some years are bad: in a bad year this portfolio could fall by about 31%, i.e. HKD 1,000,000 could become about HKD 690,000 on paper, and in a severe bear market the fall from peak to bottom could be about 40–45% (roughly HKD 550,000–600,000).
5. **The costs are small but real:** about 0.06% a year in fund fees (about HKD 550 a year on HKD 1,000,000); 30% of every US dividend is taken by the US tax authority before it reaches you; and if you die while holding US-listed funds, the US charges inheritance tax on anything above USD 60,000 (about HKD 470,000) — we plan around that in §6.3.

The test: can you say *"I will not sell in a panic if my HKD 1,000,000 becomes HKD 690,000 and stays there for two to four years"*? If yes, this plan fits you. If not, tell me and we will raise the safe part to 20–30%.

**Words used in this document:** *ETF* — a basket of many companies bought as one unit. *Expense ratio* — the fund's annual fee, taken from the fund's assets. *Treasury bill* — a short-term loan to the US government. *Yield* — the income an investment pays per year as a % of its price. *Forward P/E* — price divided by the next 12 months' expected earnings; *trailing P/E* — price divided by the last 12 months' earnings. *Drawdown / peak-to-trough* — the fall from a high point to the following low. *Rebalancing* — selling a little of what has gone up to buy what has gone down, to get back to target weights. *Withholding tax* — tax taken at source before a dividend reaches you. *US-situs asset* — an asset located in the US for US inheritance-tax purposes. *UCITS* — an Ireland/Luxembourg-domiciled fund, not a US asset. *SIPC* — the US broker-insolvency protection scheme. *Dollar-cost averaging* — investing in instalments instead of all at once. *Momentum* — the recent direction of prices.

## 2. What we are trying to achieve (investment objective)

| Item | Decision |
|---|---|
| Objective | Long-term capital growth (not income) |
| Horizon | 10+ years; money not needed before 2036 |
| Illustrative long-run assumption | ~7–9% p.a. in USD over a full cycle — **not a target and not a forecast**. We cannot and do not promise any return; you may end with less than you put in. |
| Risk budget | Willing to accept a peak-to-trough fall of ~40–45% in a severe bear market |
| Liquidity | Daily-traded ETFs; ~10% held in T-bills for flexibility |
| Constraints | No leverage, no options, no single stocks initially, no ESG screen |
| Benchmark | 90% S&P 500 + 10% US 3-month T-bills, rebalanced annually |

**Why equity-heavy at 25:** over the last 20 years the S&P 500 returned **+10.9% a year** versus **+2.8%** for 10-year Treasuries; over 30 years, **+10.3%** versus **+3.9%** (Damodaran/NYU Stern, 1928–2025, updated Jan 2026). A 25-year-old with no near-term cash need is the one investor who can genuinely afford to own that.

**You asked for US stocks — 82% of this portfolio is US equity.** The 8% developed ex-US (VEA) and 10% T-bills (SGOV) are deliberate risk-reduction deviations. Say the word and we will make it 100% US equity.

| S&P 500 trailing P/E | **26.0x** (was 29.1x a year ago) | ChartRow/SPY, 18 Nov 2026 |

| US 10-year Treasury | **5.07%** | DealPlexus, 19 Nov 2026; FT cross-check |
| HKD/USD | ~**7.85** (weak side of the 7.75–7.85 peg band) | RateMorph, 19 Nov 2026 |

**Three conclusions that drive the design:**

- **On our reading, valuation is not extreme on forward earnings but is demanding on trailing earnings.** The two P/Es differ because the market expects earnings to grow: 17.6x forward versus 26.0x trailing means a lot of future growth is already priced in. The forward multiple has fallen materially during 2026 while the index rose (Motley Fool, 16 Nov 2026 — single source, treat as indicative). The case therefore rests on **earnings delivery**, not on the multiple expanding further. Hence: own broad earnings as the core, not just the most expensive theme.
- **A 5.07% risk-free 10-year Treasury is a real competitor.** A 100% stock portfolio gives up a genuine ~5% yield. A 10% short-dated Treasury sleeve is cheap insurance and lets you rebalance into a crash.
- **Short-term momentum is negative (−4.3% in a month).** Historically lump-sum investing wins about two-thirds of the time (Vanguard, 2023 update), but the behavioural cost of a bad first month is real. We therefore **phase the entry**. Waiting for a better price is also a decision, and historically it has cost more than it saved — which is why we phase rather than wait.

Total: **HKD 1,000,000 ≈ USD 128,000** at a planning rate of 7.81 HKD/USD (spot ~7.85 on 19 Nov 2026, which would give ~USD 127,400 — the USD column is approximate).

### 4.2 Why this mix, in one paragraph

Cheap broad core (VOO+VTI = 60% of the portfolio, 0.03% fee); a growth tilt deliberately capped at 15% because we cannot predict whether AI-era earnings justify current prices; 15% in small caps and non-US markets, the only sleeves that do not move with the mega-cap technology block; 10% in T-bills as dry powder and a ~3.6% yield; and a phased entry so the first bad month does not make you quit.

Sleeve by sleeve:

- **45% VOO — the core.** The cheapest, most liquid way to own the earnings of corporate America at 0.03%. At 17.6x forward earnings it is not stretched versus its own recent history. This is the position we expect you to be able to hold through a bad market — but only you can decide that.
- **15% VTI — completeness.** VTI holds the S&P 500 *plus* ~3,000 smaller companies. Note the overlap: VTI is ~85% S&P 500 constituents, so VOO + VTI together are effectively one 60% large-cap position, not two independent bets.
- **15% QQQM — the growth tilt, sized deliberately small.** The Nasdaq-100 is 68.5% technology and 16.5% consumer discretionary (ICB, 1 Jul 2026). It returned +53.8%, +24.9% and +20.2% in 2023–2025 (≈+131% cumulative; Wikipedia/Nasdaq, retrieved 24 Oct 2026) and lost ~83% peak-to-trough in 2000–2002. **These are past returns, shown to explain why the tilt is capped at 15%, not because we expect them to repeat.** At 30% it would dominate your outcome.
- **7% VB — small caps.** Small caps are the cheapest part of the US market and the most domestically exposed. The historical small-cap premium has not worked for long stretches, including much of the last 15 years — this is a diversification sleeve, not a return promise.
- **8% VEA — outside the US.** Non-US developed markets trade at lower multiples and give you earnings from Europe, Japan and Asia-Pacific. You asked for US stocks; 8% is the minimum we would hold outside the US for diversification — say if you want it at zero. (Whether VEA is outside US estate tax depends on how you hold it — confirm with a tax adviser; see §6.3.)
- **10% SGOV — the spare tyre.** Yielding ~3.6% (18 Nov 2026) with very low price risk — but not zero: it is not a deposit, is not deposit-protected, and its yield moves with US rates. It funds rebalancing: if stocks fall 30%, this sleeve plus new savings is what you buy with.

### 4.3 Entry plan (phasing)

| Tranche | Timing | Amount | What to buy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Week 1 | HKD 500,000 (50%) | Full target weights |
| 2 | Month 2 | HKD 125,000 (12.5%) | Full target weights |
| 3 | Month 4 | HKD 125,000 (12.5%) | Full target weights |
| 4 | Month 6 | HKD 250,000 (25%) | Full target weights; top up any sleeve below target |

- Convert the **whole HKD 1,000,000 to USD up front** and hold tranches 2–4 in **SGOV**, not in HKD deposits — so the FX decision is made once and the money is already in the asset you intend to own.
- **If the market falls more than 10% before tranche 4, bring tranche 4 forward.**
- **The cost of phasing:** in a rising market this is expected to cost roughly 1–2% of terminal value versus investing everything on day one (Vanguard, 2023). We accept it because the alternative — selling in a panic — costs far more.
- **FX:** ask your broker for the all-in rate. On HKD 1,000,000 a 0.5% spread costs HKD 5,000, so it is worth comparing two brokers; convert in one or two large tickets.

### 4.4 Rules to live by

- **Rebalance when a sleeve is more than 25% away from its target weight** (e.g. VEA outside 6–10%, VOO outside 34–56%), and at least once a year. Rebalancing is what forces you to sell what has run and buy what has fallen.
- **Check quarterly, rebalance annually.** Add new savings monthly into whichever sleeve is furthest below target.
- **Do not add leverage, options or single stocks** until you have held this portfolio through one full drawdown.
- **Selling:** all six funds trade daily on US exchanges; a sale settles in about two business days, and US market hours are Hong Kong night-time.

## 6. Risks — the honest list

### 6.1 Market risk (the big one)
A 90/10 portfolio has historically fallen about **−31% in its worst calendar year** (2008: HKD 1,000,000 → ~HKD 690,000) and about **−31% in total over 2000–2002** (three years, not one). Those are calendar-year figures and **understate intra-year troughs**: the S&P 500 itself fell −56.8% peak-to-trough in 2007–09, so a 90/10 mix should be assumed to fall **~40–45% peak-to-trough** in a severe bear market (≈HKD 550,000–600,000). That is the number to test yourself against. About one calendar year in four has been negative since 1928, and intra-year drawdowns average ~−14% even in years that finish up (approximate; statisticsfundamentals.com, 3 Sep 2026). On an S&P 500 total-return basis the index took ~5.5 years to regain its 2007 high; the Nasdaq-100 took ~15 years to regain its 2000 high.

### 6.2 Concentration risk (the specific one for this portfolio)
The S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 are dominated by a handful of mega-cap technology companies; QQQM is 68.5% technology. If AI-related earnings disappoint, the 45% + 15% + 15% core-plus-tech block (75% of the portfolio) moves together. The Nasdaq-100 fell ~83% peak-to-trough in 2000–2002 (price index, approximate) and took until 2015 to regain its high. **This is why the tech tilt is capped at 15% and why 15% sits in VB/VEA — the only sleeves that do not move with the mega-cap tech block.** (VTI overlaps VOO heavily, so it is not a diversifier against large-cap tech.)

### 6.3 Tax and estate risk (the one people forget)
- **30% US dividend withholding on the US-listed ETFs** (VOO, VTI, QQQM, VB, SGOV). There is no US–Hong Kong tax treaty, so 30% of every distribution is withheld at source (Taxes for Expats, 27 Aug 2026; TIN Compliance, 26 Oct 2026). Arithmetic: ≈1.1% portfolio yield × 30% ≈ **0.33% a year ≈ HKD 3,300**. VEA's dividends suffer *foreign* withholding at source instead (typically ~15%), not the 30% US rate. HK does not tax dividends or capital gains, so there is no further layer.
- **US estate tax above USD 60,000.** A non-US person who dies owning US-listed securities gets only a **USD 60,000** exemption; the excess is taxed at graduated rates up to **40%**, with Form 706-NA required (IRS, 27 Jun 2026). Your ~USD 128,000 portfolio is ~USD 68,000 above the threshold — roughly **USD 18,000–27,000 (≈HKD 140,000–210,000) of tax on death, up to ~20% of the portfolio** — and the exposure grows with the portfolio (by year 10 the planning case is ~USD 190,000 above the threshold).
- **Mitigations:** (i) consider Ireland-domiciled UCITS equivalents for part of the core — not US-situs, and typically 15% US dividend withholding under the US–Ireland treaty, worth roughly 0.15%–0.2% a year on this portfolio; this only helps if the US-listed ETFs are *replaced*, not added to, and UCITS equivalents typically cost 0.07–0.20% versus 0.03%, with possibly higher HK broker fees for LSE/HK-listed lines; (ii) hold the non-US sleeve (VEA) — but confirm with a tax adviser whether buying it through a US broker changes its treatment; (iii) if this becomes material we will refer you to a tax adviser — we do not give tax advice.

### 6.4 Currency risk (small, under the peg)
The HKD is pegged at **7.75–7.85** per USD, a total range of ~1.3%. At today's 7.85 (the weak edge) the only FX move available is HKD appreciation of up to ~1.3% if the peg strengthens to 7.75. So the real risks are drawdown, tax and estate, not the exchange rate. Two caveats: HKD interest rates track US rates, so HKD cash also yields ~3–4%; and the peg has held since 1983 but is a policy choice, not a law of nature — a break or a widening of the band would be a material event for you and is not covered by this plan.

### 6.5 Rate and valuation risk
The 10-year Treasury at **5.07%** is the number to watch. Higher long-term yields lower the present value of long-duration growth stocks and make the 10% cash sleeve more attractive relative to stocks. If the 10-year moves above ~5.5–6%, we would consider raising the cash/bond sleeve to 15%.

### 6.6 Behavioural risk (the one that actually costs money)
The biggest threat to this plan is you selling in a panic. A 25-year-old who sells at the bottom of a −40% drawdown converts a temporary loss into a permanent one. The phased entry, the 10% cash sleeve and the rebalancing rule exist to make staying invested easier.

### 6.7 Custody and broker risk
Your assets sit with a broker, not with us. Use a broker with segregated client accounts and SIPC-type protection (US SIPC covers up to USD 500,000, of which USD 250,000 cash), keep your own records, and consider splitting custody above ~USD 250,000. ETF assets are held by the fund's own custodian, not by your broker.

### 6.8 Life-event risk (your own life)
This plan assumes you will not need the money for 10+ years. A 25-year-old may need it for a house deposit, relocation or a job loss. **If you might need this money within five years, it does not belong in this portfolio** — keep 6–12 months of expenses in HKD cash outside this plan, and tell me so we can cut the equity share.

### 6.9 What this plan does *not* protect against
Inflation over 10+ years (stocks are a partial, not complete, hedge), a US market that underperforms for a decade (possible — 2000–2009 was roughly flat), a US default or political shock, ETF closure or tracking difference, or a change to the HKD peg.

## 7. Trade-offs we made (and the alternatives)

| Decision | Why | What we gave up |
|---|---|---|
| 90% equities, not 100% | 5.07% risk-free yield; rebalancing ammunition; sleep | ~1–2% p.a. of expected return in a strong bull market |
| 90/10, not 70/30 | 10+ year horizon; you can tolerate a 40% fall | At 70/30 the bad-year fall would be roughly −22% instead of −31% |
| 15% tech tilt, not 30% | Concentration risk; 2000–02 precedent | Upside if AI earnings keep beating |
| 8% developed ex-US | Valuation, diversification | Recent relative underperformance vs US |
| Phased entry over 6 months | Behavioural; negative 1-month momentum | Historically lump sum wins ~2/3 of the time; ~1–2% of terminal value in a rising market |
| ETFs, not single stocks | Diversification, 0.06% cost, no stock-picking risk | No chance of a 10-bagger |
| No leverage | A 40% drawdown with leverage is a wipeout | Lower headline return |

We accept these costs because the alternative — selling in a panic — costs far more.

## 8. Monitoring and review

- **Monthly:** add savings; check nothing has drifted more than 25% relative to target.
- **Quarterly:** read one page of market data (valuation, 10-year yield, Fed rate) — not daily prices.
- **Annually:** rebalance; review whether the horizon or risk tolerance has changed; revisit the estate-tax structure as the portfolio grows past USD 60,000.
- **Triggers to revisit the plan:** 10-year Treasury above ~5.5–6%; a drawdown beyond −45%; a change in your job, housing or family plans; the portfolio passing ~USD 250,000 (estate-tax planning becomes material).
