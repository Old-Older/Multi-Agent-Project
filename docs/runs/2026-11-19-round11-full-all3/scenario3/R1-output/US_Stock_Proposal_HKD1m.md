## 1. Introduction — in plain language (read this first)

You have HKD 1,000,000 — about USD 127,000, because the Hong Kong dollar is kept at roughly 7.8 to the US dollar. You are 25, you will not need this money for many years, and you told me you can sit through a big fall without selling. That combination — long time, strong stomach — is the single biggest advantage you have as an investor. It lets you own shares, which have grown the most over long periods, instead of hiding in cash.

Here is the whole plan in five sentences:

1. **Most of your money (about 70%) goes into one or two very cheap, very broad US index funds** — you own hundreds of companies at once, so no single company can ruin you — **because at 25 you have time to wait out the bad years, and shares have beaten cash over long periods.**
2. **About 15% goes into a growth/technology fund** because you are young and can accept more ups and downs in exchange for a chance at more growth — but this is the part that will hurt most in a bad year.
3. **About 10% goes into smaller and cheaper-valued US companies** — **because they are priced much lower than the big tech names (P/E 11 vs 22), so they cushion you if tech falls.**
4. **About 5% stays in short-term US Treasury bills** — a cash-like reserve that currently pays about 3.6% a year, so you have money to buy more if the market falls a lot.
5. **You invest in stages over about 6 months, not all at once**, and you rebalance once a year. You never borrow to invest.

**What you must expect:** the value will fall sometimes, a lot. Expect a fall of 30–40% at some point, and be prepared for a severe bear market of about 50% (HKD 1,000,000 → about HKD 500,000 on paper) — that is what happened in 2000–02 and 2007–09 to a portfolio like this. Put differently: in a bad year your statement will show a loss bigger than a year's salary, and the recovery has historically taken anywhere from a few months to about six years. **That is the normal price of the higher long-run return — it is not a sign that something has gone wrong with your plan.** The plan only works if you keep buying and do not sell in a panic.

> **Words used in this document, in plain English**
> - **ETF (exchange-traded fund):** a basket of many companies' shares that trades like one share on a stock exchange. Buying one ETF = buying a slice of hundreds of companies.
> - **Index fund:** an ETF that simply copies a list of companies (an "index") instead of a manager picking them.
> - **TER (total expense ratio):** the yearly fee the fund charges, already taken out of the price. 0.07% = HKD 7 a year per HKD 10,000 invested.
> - **Drawdown:** how far the value falls from its highest point. A "30–40% drawdown" = HKD 1,000,000 temporarily becoming HKD 600,000–700,000.
> - **Rebalancing:** once a year, selling a little of what has grown and buying a little of what has shrunk, to get back to the planned percentages.
> - **P/E (price-to-earnings):** the price you pay per HKD 1 of yearly company profit. Higher = you are paying more for each dollar of profit.
> - **CAPE:** the same idea as P/E but averaged over 10 years, to smooth out booms and busts. It is a long-run "expensive or cheap?" gauge.
> - **Beta:** how much a fund moves when the market moves. Beta 1.24 ≈ it tends to move about 24% more than the market, up and down.
> - **UCITS:** an EU-regulated fund label. Irish UCITS funds are the tax-efficient way for a Hong Kong resident to own US shares (see §6.2).
> - **W-8BEN:** a one-page form you sign at your broker to confirm you are not a US taxpayer, so you are taxed at the correct rate.
> - **Satellite:** a smaller side bet next to the main core holdings.
> - **AUM:** total money in the fund — bigger usually means easier to buy and sell.
> - **bps (basis points):** 1 bp = 0.01%. 20 bps = 0.20%.

Notes on instrument choice:
- **Sleeve 2 must hold only companies outside the S&P 500 (VXF/VB). Do not use VTI here — it duplicates the core** (VTI is ~85% the same stocks as the S&P 500, so 55% S&P 500 + 15% VTI would give ~68% effective S&P 500 and almost no genuine small-cap exposure).

Holding the core and growth sleeves in Irish UCITS ETFs (CSPX/VUAA for the S&P 500; a UCITS Nasdaq-100 fund for sleeve 3) removes that exposure; UCITS small-cap-value options exist but are **not verified in this document** — check before switching.
- **Accumulating share classes** (CSPX, VUAA) reinvest dividends automatically — simpler for a growth investor and no investor-level withholding.
- **If you want individual stocks**, use up to 10% of the portfolio (max 3% per stock) taken from the QQQM sleeve, not on top of it; otherwise the 10% small-cap-value ETF is the side bet (satellite).

### Staged entry (to reduce timing risk)
| Month | Amount invested | What |
|---|---|---|
| Month 0 | 40% (HKD 400,000) | Core + cash sleeve |
| Month 2 | 20% (HKD 200,000) | Core + growth |
| Month 4 | 20% (HKD 200,000) | Core + small-cap value |
| Month 6 | 20% (HKD 200,000) | Complete all sleeves to target weights |

Rationale: the S&P 500 is ~6% below its 6 Oct 2026 record of 7,818.93 and was on a 5-day losing streak with VIX at 24.83 on 18 Nov 2026 (AP; Titan Protect). Staging does not beat lump-sum investing on average, but it reduces the regret of buying everything the week before a fall — a behavioural benefit worth more than the small expected cost for a first-time investor.
**Cost of staging:** on average about 30% of the money is in cash for six months, which costs roughly 1–1.5% of expected return if markets rise — a deliberate trade for lower regret. The staged cash is separate from the 5% SGOV sleeve; do not count it twice. If you are comfortable with volatility, investing 100% immediately is statistically better on average.

### Rebalancing and rules
- **Rebalance once a year** (or when any sleeve drifts more than ±5 percentage points) back to target weights. This mechanically sells what has run up and buys what has fallen.
- **No leverage, no margin, no options.**
- **Side-bet (satellite) cap:** individual stocks (if you add any) never exceed 10% of the portfolio in total, and no single stock above 3%.
- **Review trigger:** re-read this document after any 20% portfolio drawdown before doing anything.

## 4. Why this allocation — the reasoning

**4.1 Why equities, and why now is not "too high".** Over the last 20 years the S&P 500 returned about **+11.2% a year** (30 years: +10.3%; 50 years: +11.9%; ~10% a year over the last century — Ben Carlson, 18 Oct 2026, through 30 Sep 2026). Cash and bonds have not come close over such periods. Your 10+ year horizon is what makes this appropriate.

**4.2 Why a broad index core rather than stock picking.** A 25-year-old with a full career ahead has no advantage in knowing which share will win, and single-stock outcomes are extremely wide: within the same "Magnificent 7" basket, 1-year returns on 18 Nov 2026 ranged from **NVDA −18.9%** to **AAPL +32.2%** — a ~51 percentage-point spread (StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026). An index fund turns that dispersion into the average, which is what you want when you cannot predict the winner.

**4.3 Why 55% in US large caps.** The US market is the deepest, most profitable and most innovative equity market, and the S&P 500 is the cheapest way to own it (0.03%–0.07% a year). It is also where the earnings growth is: forward EPS estimates rose 33.6% year-to-date while the index rose 7.4%, which pushed the forward P/E to **17.7x — below its 10-year average of 19.1x** (FactSet, 5 Nov 2026). But on forward earnings the market looks fairly valued (17.7x vs a 19.1x 10-year average), while on the long-run CAPE measure (**38.6 vs a 17.5 mean**) it looks expensive. The two disagree because forward estimates assume a 33.6% jump in earnings; if those estimates are cut, the "cheap" reading disappears. **Do not read either one as a reason to feel safe — the plan is built to survive being wrong about valuation.**

**4.4 Why 15% growth/tech on top.** You are 25 with a 10+ year horizon and high risk tolerance; a Nasdaq-100 sleeve (QQQM, 0.15%) has returned **+20.19% a year over 10 years** versus +15.20% for the S&P 500 (PortfoliosLab, 14 Nov 2026). That extra return came with a beta of 1.24 and a top-10 weight of 46.2% (StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026) — it is a concentrated bet on the same mega-cap tech names, so it is capped at 15%. **Over the last 12 months this sleeve returned +9.81% versus +10.95% for the S&P 500 — the extra long-run return is not guaranteed and can lag for years.** QQQM's top-10 (46.2%) overlaps heavily with the core; it is capped at 15% for that reason.

**4.5 Why 10% small-cap value.** Small-cap value has historically earned a premium over large-cap growth over long periods, and it is the sleeve least correlated with the mega-cap tech that dominates the core: AVUV trades at a P/E of **11.09** versus **22.13** for VOO (StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026). It is the cheapest valuation exposure in the plan and the natural cushion against a fall in the price of expensive tech shares.

**4.6 Why 5% cash.** SGOV yields **3.63%** with ~1-month duration (StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026) — a real return versus HKD cash, near-zero price risk, and the psychological anchor that lets you buy during a crash instead of selling. It is USD-denominated and earns USD rates.

**4.7 Why not more diversification (international, bonds)?** You asked for a US-stock portfolio. A 100% US equity book is a concentrated bet on one country's market, and US valuations are high on the long-run CAPE measure. If you later want to reduce that, the first change I would make is to move 10–20% of the core into ex-US equities (VXUS, 0.05%, P/E 14.39) — not into bonds, which at your horizon mainly reduce expected return.

## 6. Risks — what can go wrong

**6.1 Market / drawdown risk (the main one).** US equities have fallen **−51% (2000–02), −57% (2007–09), −34% (2020) and −25% (2022)**, with recoveries taking from 5 months to ~6 years (Plus500, 22 Jul 2026; FPA, 2022). Since 1950 there have been ~38 corrections of more than 10% and ~14 bear markets of more than 20% (Plus500). **On this portfolio (95% equities) a repeat of 2000–02 (−51%) or 2007–09 (−57%) would mean roughly −48% to −54%, i.e. HKD 1,000,000 → HKD 460,000–520,000, with a recovery that historically took 2–6 years.** The QQQM sleeve (beta 1.24) makes it worse than the index in a tech-led sell-off. The plan assumes you do not sell at that point.

**6.2 Tax and domicile risk (specific to a Hong Kong investor).**
- **US dividend withholding tax: 30%** for a Hong Kong resident (no US–HK tax treaty), versus **15%** inside an Irish-domiciled UCITS ETF. On a ~1.1% dividend yield, 30% US withholding costs about **0.33% of portfolio value a year**; an Irish UCITS fund pays 15% inside the fund, about **0.17%** — a saving of roughly **0.17% a year**, still larger than the 0.04% fee gap (0.03% vs 0.07%) (TradeSmart, 2 Apr 2026; Saxo, 30 Aug 2026; arithmetic from stated yields).
- **US estate tax:** the exemption for a non-resident alien is only **USD 60,000** of US-situs assets, taxed at 18%–40% above that, and Hong Kong has **no US estate-tax treaty** (USTAXFS, 2026). Assets above USD 60,000 are taxed on a progressive 18–40% scale; on ~USD 67,000 above the exemption that is roughly **USD 14,000–15,000 (about 11–12% of the portfolio)** in the worst case — illustrative, not a filed-liability estimate, and very unlikely at 25. **The simple fix is to hold the core in Irish-domiciled UCITS funds, which sit outside US estate tax entirely** — that is why §3 recommends them.
- **SGOV's Treasury interest is generally not subject to US withholding for a non-resident** — the 30% applies to dividends, not to T-bill interest.
- **Action:** file a **W-8BEN** with your broker; prefer Irish-domiciled accumulating ETFs for the core.

**6.3 Concentration risk.** The S&P 500 is not 500 equal bets: the **top 10 stocks are ~38.6%** of the index and the Magnificent 7 ~32–34% (invest-like, 2 Nov 2026; Economic Times, 9 Nov 2026 — secondary sources, figures differ). In VOO, **AAPL 7.70% + NVDA 7.58% + MSFT 5.93% = 21.2% in three stocks** (StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026). Across the whole portfolio, about **32% sits in the 10 largest US stocks and about 27–28% in the Magnificent 7** — the core, the completion sleeve and QQQM all hold the same names. A single AI-capex disappointment would hit roughly a third of your money at once.

**6.4 Valuation risk.** CAPE at **38.63** is ~2.2x its long-run mean and second only to the 1999–2000 peak (multpl.com, 18 Nov 2026). High starting valuations have historically meant lower 10–20 year returns. Note the conflict: forward P/E (17.7x) says "fairly valued", CAPE says "expensive" — the gap depends on forward earnings estimates being met.

**6.5 Interest-rate risk.** The 10-year Treasury yields **5.07%** (multpl.com, 18 Nov 2026) and October 2026 FOMC minutes showed "almost all" officials expecting further tightening (Moneycontrol, 18 Nov 2026). Higher rates pressure growth shares, whose value depends on profits far in the future — precisely the growth sleeve — and make the 5% cash sleeve a real competitor to equities.

**6.6 Currency risk.** The HKD is pegged at 7.75–7.85 (HKMA), so day-to-day FX risk is small (~1.3% band width). But your salary and spending are in HKD, so you are betting on the US dollar as well as on US shares; the real risk is a change in the peg regime, not daily volatility. Converting near 7.85 (spot ~7.845 on 16 Nov 2026) is at the cheap end for USD buyers.

**6.7 Liquidity and execution risk.** Low for the US-listed ETFs (VOO $1.05T, VTI $692B, QQQM $105B AUM — StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026). **The Irish UCITS core (CSPX/VUAA) trades on the London Stock Exchange; check that your broker offers the LSE line and use limit orders — spreads are wider than on US exchanges.** Costs are small but real: IBKR commission ~USD 0.005/share (min USD 1) and FX spread ~2 bps, versus 8–20 bps at some HK retail brokers — **HKD 200 vs HKD 2,000 on HKD 1,000,000** (IBKR HK, 3 May 2026; TradeSmart, 3 Jul 2026). Use one FX conversion, not many small ones.

**6.8 Behavioural risk (the biggest one for a 25-year-old).** The plan fails if you sell in a panic, chase a hot theme, or check the portfolio daily. Rules: annual rebalancing only, no leverage, individual stocks capped at 10%.

**6.9 Model and data risk.** This is a simulated proposal built on secondary sources dated 18 Nov 2026; several figures are flagged unverified in the research files (e.g. Nasdaq-100 price-basis YTD, top-10 weight 38.6% vs 40.4%, BIL fee, QQQ's own 1-year return, UCITS Nasdaq-100 and small-cap-value equivalents). Re-check prices and tax rules before acting.

**6.10 Country risk.** 100% of the equity is US — a US-specific shock (policy, dollar, earnings) hits everything at once. The first diversifier to add later is ex-US equity (VXUS, 0.05%, P/E 14.4 vs 22.1 for VOO).

**6.11 AI / semiconductor risk.** Semiconductors are already top-5 weights in the core (NVDA 7.58%, AVGO 2.37%, MU 1.53%, AMD 1.26% of VOO — StockAnalysis, 18 Nov 2026) and NVDA fell 18.9% in the last year; a capex slowdown would hit the core and QQQM together.
