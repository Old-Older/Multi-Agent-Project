# Your Simulated US-Stock Plan — Explained Simply

**Who this is for:** a 25-year-old in Hong Kong, HKD 1,000,000 to invest, no finance background, no prior experience with US stocks or ETFs.
**What this is:** a *simulation* — a practice run for learning. It is not real advice and no real money is being moved. (More on that at the end.)

---

## 1. What we are proposing

We would put your HKD 1,000,000 into **six low-cost funds** (called ETFs — think of them as ready-made baskets of many companies, explained in the FAQ) and then leave them alone for a long time. Roughly **70% goes into US stocks** (about HKD 700,000), **15% into stocks outside the US** (about HKD 150,000), and **15% into bonds and cash-like holdings** (about HKD 150,000) as a cushion. The six baskets are: VOO (40%, the 500 biggest US companies), VTI (20%, essentially the entire US market including smaller companies), VEA (15%, developed markets outside the US such as Europe and Japan), QQQ (10%, the 100 largest mostly-tech companies on the Nasdaq), BND (7.5%, US bonds) and SGOV (7.5%, very short-term US government bills — basically a parking space for cash that earns a little interest). The **fund fees** on these baskets add up to about **0.05% a year, roughly HKD 500** — less than a nice dinner for two. Your broker will also charge commission and a currency-conversion fee when you buy, and US-listed funds withhold 30% of dividends (see section 5), so the true all-in cost is a little higher than HKD 500.

## 2. Why we split it that way

You have probably heard "don't put all your eggs in one basket." That is the whole idea. If one basket breaks, you still have eggs.

The US stock market is the biggest and most successful in the world, which is why it gets the largest share. But two things make us careful. First, it is **expensive** right now: as of November 2026, investors are paying about 17.7 times next year's expected earnings for the S&P 500, and a longer-term measure (the "CAPE", which smooths profits over 10 years) is around 37–39 — historically high. Second, it is **top-heavy**: seven giant tech companies (the "Magnificent 7") make up roughly a third of the whole index. If those seven stumble, a "diversified" US fund stumbles with them.

So instead of betting on a handful of famous names, we own **the whole market** (VTI and VOO together cover big and small US companies — note they overlap a lot, since VTI already contains the 500 big ones, so this is really one big US holding rather than two separate bets), add **some overseas** (VEA) so we are not 100% dependent on one country, and keep **a safety cushion** (BND and SGOV) so that when stocks fall we have something steady to lean on and something to rebalance with. The small QQQ slice is a deliberate tilt toward growth — it adds extra ups and downs, which suits your long horizon and high risk tolerance, but it is kept at 10% so it cannot sink the ship.

## 3. What could go wrong — in plain numbers

This is the part most people skip, so let's be blunt.

- **A bad year:** a drop of about 30% is entirely normal for a portfolio like this. That is HKD 1,000,000 becoming about **HKD 700,000**.
- **A really bad year:** in a severe crash — like 2008 or 2000–2002 — this could fall to roughly **HKD 550,000**. The US market has fallen 44% in a single calendar year (1931), about 49% peak-to-trough in 2000–2002, and about 57% in 2007–2009.
- **Recovery:** markets have usually recovered, but not always quickly. In the two worst modern cases it took about **5.5 years (2007–09) and 7 years (2000–02)** to get back to even; the typical recovery has been much shorter, around 2 years. Some markets have taken far longer — Japan's market in 1989 and the Nasdaq in 2000 took 15–25 years. So plan for **5–7 years** as a realistic bad case, and understand that "it always comes back" is not a law of nature.

**That is normal and expected, not a mistake.** A fall is the price of admission for the higher long-run returns that stocks have delivered (roughly 10% a year before inflation over the very long run — but that is an average across decades, not a promise for any one year, and past results never guarantee future ones). If you sell in the middle of a fall, you turn a temporary paper loss into a permanent one.

## 4. The three things you must actually DO

Everything else is detail. These three matter most.

1. **Open a broker account.** Choose a regulated broker that lets Hong Kong residents buy US-listed ETFs, check the fees (commission, currency conversion, custody), and make sure you understand how to fund it in HKD and convert to USD. Take your time; compare two or three.
2. **Spread the buying over 6–12 months.** Do not put all HKD 1,000,000 in on one day. Buy in equal monthly chunks (about HKD 83,000–167,000 a month). This is called "dollar-cost averaging" — it means you buy more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high, and it protects you from the bad luck of buying everything the day before a crash.
3. **Do not panic-sell.** Decide now, in writing, that you will not sell during a crash. Your horizon is 10+ years; the only thing that can really hurt you is your own reaction. If anything, a big fall is when you would consider adding a little extra.

## 5. The one thing most people miss: US estate tax

Here is something almost no beginner is told. If you hold **US-listed** ETFs (like VOO, VTI, QQQ) and you are not a US citizen, the US tax authority treats those holdings as US property. Above **USD 60,000** (about HKD 470,000), they can charge estate tax of **up to 40%** if you die — and Hong Kong has no tax treaty with the US to soften this. In your case, roughly **USD 68,000** of the portfolio would sit above that threshold (about HKD 530,000 of US-listed holdings minus the USD 60,000 allowance), and the worst-case tax on that slice is about **USD 27,000, or roughly HKD 210,000**. There is also a 30% withholding tax on dividends paid to Hong Kong investors.

**The simple fix:** there are **Irish-domiciled** versions of the same ideas — for example **CSPX** (S&P 500), **VUAA** (S&P 500, accumulating) and **VWRA** (all-world). They track similar markets, are not US property so the US estate tax does not apply, and they pay only **15%** dividend withholding instead of 30%. The trade-offs are slightly higher fees and, for some, less liquidity. For a long-term Hong Kong investor, many people choose the Irish versions for exactly this reason. This is a genuine decision point — worth discussing with a tax adviser before any real money moves.

## 6. Beginner FAQ

**What is an ETF?**
A basket that holds many companies at once, and you buy a slice of the basket like a single share. One purchase of VTI gives you thousands of US companies. It is cheap, easy to buy and sell, and you do not have to pick winners.

**Why not just buy Apple or Tesla?**
Because one company can fall 50% and never come back — think of famous names that dominated a decade and then faded. Owning the whole market means you never have to guess which company wins; you own the winner automatically, and the losers are too small to hurt you.

**Why not wait for a crash?**
Because nobody knows when it will come, and markets often rise a lot before they fall. Waiting is itself a bet, and it is a bet that has cost many people years of gains. Spreading your buying over 6–12 months is the honest compromise: you are protected from bad timing without trying to predict the future.

**What if the market drops right after I buy?**
It might. That is not a sign you did something wrong — it is the normal behaviour of markets. Because you are buying monthly, your later purchases will buy the same things cheaper, which helps you recover faster. This is exactly why we do not invest in one go.

**Do I need to watch it every day?**
No — and you are better off not doing so. Daily prices are noise. Check in once or twice a year to rebalance (bring the percentages back to target) and to confirm nothing has changed in your life. Watching every day mostly increases the chance you will do something impulsive.
