# Simulated US Equity Investment Proposal
**Client profile:** Age 25, Hong Kong resident, HKD 1,000,000, 10+ year horizon, growth objective, moderately high risk tolerance, ETFs + a few large-cap stocks, lump-sum entry phased over 6 months.
**Status: SIMULATED / EDUCATIONAL.** This is a paper portfolio for learning and discussion. It is not a real trade instruction, not a guarantee, and not personalised regulated advice. All figures are illustrative.

---

## 1. Investment objective

Grow HKD 1,000,000 into a long-term core holding over 10+ years, accepting meaningful short-term ups and downs in exchange for higher expected long-run returns. Secondary objective: build investing habits and knowledge, so the portfolio is deliberately simple, low-cost and easy to monitor.

**Return expectation (not a promise):** roughly 6–8% per year in USD terms over a full market cycle — meaning over a decade or more, not any single year. Honest caveat: starting valuations are high (§4), so the next decade is more likely to land at the low end of that range than the high end. Some years will be +25%, some −30%.

**What this portfolio is not for:** money you may need within 3–5 years (a house deposit, tuition, an emergency fund). Keep those in HKD cash or short-dated T-bills separately.

## 2. Target allocation

| Sleeve | Weight | HKD | Instrument (simulated) | What it actually holds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Core US broad market** | 40% | 400,000 | VOO (S&P 500, 0.03%) | The 500 largest US companies, weighted by size |
| **US growth tilt** | 12% | 120,000 | SCHG (US large-cap growth, 0.04%) | Large US companies with faster expected profit growth |
| **US small & mid cap** | 10% | 100,000 | IJR (0.06%) + IJH (0.05%), 50/50 | Smaller US companies (roughly ranks 500–1,100 by size) |
| **Developed ex-US** | 10% | 100,000 | VEA (0.03%) | Large companies in Europe, Japan, Australia, Canada |
| **Emerging markets** | 5% | 50,000 | VWO (0.07%) | Companies in China, India, Taiwan, Brazil, etc. |
| **Individual large-cap stocks** | 10% | 100,000 | 4–5 names, ~2–2.5% each (§3) | Hand-picked companies — the learning sleeve |
| **Bonds** | 8% | 80,000 | BND (0.03%) + SHY (0.15%), 50/50 | Loans to US government and companies; steadier than shares |
| **Cash / T-bills** | 5% | 50,000 | SGOV (0.07%) | Very short-term US government debt; near-cash |
| **Total** | **100%** | **1,000,000** | | |

**Summary: ~77% shares / 8% bonds / 5% cash.** Of the shares, ~62% is US and ~15% is outside the US.
**Blended ETF cost ≈ 0.05% a year**, i.e. roughly HKD 500 per year on HKD 1m. Cost is not the enemy here; behaviour is.

### Why this shape
- **The US is the core, but not the whole world.** The US is ~60–65% of global stock-market value and the deepest, cheapest market to access from Hong Kong. But a 100% US portfolio is really a bet on a handful of giant technology companies. 15% outside the US and 10% in smaller US companies is the cheapest insurance against that concentration.
- **The growth tilt is small on purpose.** SCHG overlaps VOO heavily (many of the same companies, bigger weights). 12% adds a tilt without doubling the same bet.
- **10% in single stocks, not 30%.** At 25 with a long horizon, picking a few stocks is a legitimate way to learn — but position size is what separates learning from gambling. A stock that goes to zero costs 2.5% of the portfolio, not 25%.
- **8% bonds + 5% cash looks low, and that is intentional.** With a 10+ year horizon, bonds mainly serve as rebalancing fuel and psychological ballast. Their expected return (4.5–5.5%) is currently competitive with shares' (see §4), which is exactly why we hold some rather than none.

## 3. The individual-stock sleeve (10%, HKD 100,000)

Rules, applied mechanically:
1. **4–5 names maximum, ~2–2.5% each.** No single stock above 3%.
2. **Only profitable, cash-generative large companies** (market value above USD 100bn, positive free cash flow — cash left after running and maintaining the business). No pre-revenue companies, no meme stocks, no leveraged or inverse products.
3. **No more than 2 names from the same sector**, and the sleeve should not simply re-buy the biggest names already inside VOO.
4. **Written thesis before buying:** one paragraph on why you own it, one on what would make you sell. If you cannot write both, do not buy.
5. **No averaging down on a broken thesis.** Add only if the thesis is intact and the price fell for non-fundamental reasons.
6. **Review quarterly, not daily.**

Illustrative (simulated) names spanning different sectors — *examples for the exercise, not recommendations*: a mega-cap platform/cloud business, a payments or financial-infrastructure business, a healthcare or consumer-staples compounder, an industrial or energy-infrastructure name. The point is sector spread, not stock tips.

## 4. Why now — and the honest counter-argument

**Supporting the allocation:**
- Profits are at record highs and still growing (S&P 500 12-month earnings ~USD 298 per index unit, +~17% year-on-year at the latest reading), so the market is expensive partly because earnings are genuinely strong.
- Long-run expected returns by building block: US large cap ~4–6%, US small/mid ~6–8%, developed ex-US ~6–8%, EM ~6–9%, bonds ~4.5–5.5%, T-bills ~3.5–4.5%. The extra expected return sits outside US large cap — which is why 25% of the portfolio is there.
- The HKD is pegged to the USD (7.75–7.85), so a Hong Kong investor does **not** face day-to-day currency swings on USD assets — a real structural advantage over, say, a euro-based investor. **But be precise about what the peg is:** it is a policy regime maintained by the HKMA, not a legal guarantee. It removes everyday translation risk; it does not remove the risk that the regime itself changes, and it means Hong Kong imports US interest-rate policy.

**Against — the part most pitches leave out:**
- **Valuation is stretched.** S&P 500 trailing P/E ~25x, Shiller CAPE ~39x versus a ~16x long-run median, price/sales and price/book at or near record highs, dividend yield ~1.1%. The **earnings yield (~4.0%) is below the 10-year Treasury yield (~5.1%)** — meaning you are currently paid less for owning shares than for lending to the US government. Historically, buying at these valuations has produced mediocre 10-year returns, though the range of outcomes is very wide.
- **Concentration risk.** A handful of giant technology companies drive a large share of index returns. If they fall out of favour, the "diversified" index falls with them.
- **This is why we phase entry over 6 months** rather than buying everything on day one. Statistically, investing the lump sum at once has won about two-thirds of the time — but phasing cuts the regret of buying right before a fall, and for a first portfolio, staying invested matters more than squeezing out the last 1%.

## 5. Implementation: phased entry over 6 months

| Month | Deploy | Into |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | HKD 200,000 | VOO 100k, VEA 40k, BND/SHY 30k, SGOV 30k |
| 2 | HKD 150,000 | VOO 60k, IJR/IJH 40k, SCHG 50k |
| 3 | HKD 150,000 | VOO 60k, VWO 30k, SCHG 30k, SGOV 30k |
| 4 | HKD 150,000 | VOO 60k, VEA 30k, IJR/IJH 30k, BND/SHY 30k |
| 5 | HKD 150,000 | VOO 60k, SCHG 40k, VWO 20k, SGOV 30k |
| 6 | HKD 200,000 | Complete all sleeves to target; start the stock sleeve (2 names, then 2–3 more) |

**On the 5% cash sleeve:** it is a *permanent* part of the target allocation, not just an entry reserve. During months 1–5 the SGOV balance temporarily runs above 5% because it holds money not yet deployed; by month 6 it settles at the 5% target and stays there as dry powder for rebalancing.

Practical notes: convert HKD→USD in 2–3 tranches to smooth the conversion; use limit orders (an order with a maximum price you set, rather than "whatever the market gives you"); expect dealing costs of roughly 0.05–0.2% per trade depending on broker; check minimum trade sizes on your broker (some charge per order, so batch small purchases). Keep a separate HKD emergency fund of 6 months' expenses that is **not** part of this HKD 1m.

## 6. Rebalancing and maintenance

- **Rebalance when a sleeve drifts more than ±5 percentage points** from target, or once a year, whichever comes first. Rebalancing is the mechanism that forces you to sell what has run up and buy what has lagged.
- **Review the stock sleeve quarterly** against the written thesis. Cut a name if the thesis breaks, not because the price fell.
- **Do not check daily.** A monthly look is plenty; the portfolio is designed to be boring.
- **Add new savings** to whichever sleeve is furthest below target — the cheapest form of rebalancing.

## 7. Risks — what can go wrong

| Risk | Likelihood (subjective, overlapping — not additive) | What it looks like | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Valuation de-rating** — high starting multiples compress | 35–45% over 10 yrs | Flat returns for years despite growing earnings; 20–30% fall | Phased entry, non-US and small-cap diversification, bonds |
| **Mega-cap tech concentration unwind** | 25–35% | Index falls 25–35% while equal-weight holds up better | 10% small/mid, 15% non-US, position caps |
| **Recession / earnings recession** | 20–25% | 35–45% peak-to-trough fall, 2–4 years to recover | 8% bonds + 5% cash, long horizon, no leverage |
| **Rate/inflation shock** (sticky inflation, higher long yields) | 20–25% | Bonds and shares fall together; real (after-inflation) returns negative | Short-duration bonds (SHY), T-bills, no long-duration bets |
| **Single-stock blow-up** in the 10% sleeve | 10–15% per name | One holding loses 50%+ | 2–2.5% position caps, sector limits, written theses |
| **Behavioural risk** — selling in a panic | The biggest risk of all | Locking in a 35% loss permanently | Pre-commitment, phased entry, plain-language plan, no daily checking |

These probabilities overlap: a valuation de-rating and a recession can happen at the same time, so do not add them up.

### 7.1 How bad can it get? A consistent ladder

| Scenario | Portfolio fall | HKD 1,000,000 becomes | Typical recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal bad year | −20% to −30% | ~700,000–800,000 | Months |
| Recession (e.g. 2001, 2022-style) | −35% to −45% | ~550,000–650,000 | 2–4 years |
| Systemic crisis (2008-style) | −45% to −55% | ~450,000–550,000 | 3–5 years |

These are estimates from the portfolio's own weights and historical equity drawdowns, not forecasts. **If the systemic row would make you sell, the share weight is too high and we should cut it before investing, not after.**

### 7.2 Risks specific to a Hong Kong investor

- **US dividend withholding tax: 30%** for non-US persons, with no HK–US tax treaty relief. This is why the portfolio tilts to low-dividend growth exposure rather than high-yield US stocks — the tax quietly eats income.
- **US estate tax — a live exposure, not a footnote.** For non-US persons, US-situs assets above roughly **USD 60,000** can be exposed to US estate tax at rates up to **40%**, with no HK–US estate-tax treaty. On a HKD 1m portfolio this is a real issue, not a theoretical one. **Mitigation:** use **Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs** instead of US-domiciled ones — e.g. CSPX or VUAA (S&P 500), IWDA (developed world), EIMI (emerging markets), AGGU or IB01 (bonds/cash). These are not US-situs for estate-tax purposes and typically suffer only **15%** US withholding on dividends under the Ireland–US treaty, versus 30%. They cost slightly more (roughly 0.07–0.20%) and are less liquid, but for a Hong Kong investor the tax saving usually outweighs that. **Confirm with a qualified tax adviser before any real-money implementation.**
- **Broker / custody risk:** use a regulated, SFC-licensed or major international broker; understand whether your assets are held in your own name and how they are protected if the broker fails.
- **The peg** means Hong Kong imports US monetary policy — good for FX certainty, less good for local rate cycles.

## 8. What "success" looks like

Not beating an index in year one. Success is: (1) you stayed invested through the first 30% fall, (2) you can explain every holding in one sentence, (3) total costs stayed under ~0.15% a year, and (4) after 10 years the portfolio has grown clearly faster than HKD cash in the bank. If those four hold, the strategy worked regardless of any single year's number.
