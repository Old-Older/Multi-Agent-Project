## 1. The one-page version (read this first)

**Words used below, in plain English:** *ETF* = a basket of many stocks that trades like one share. *Expense ratio* = the yearly fee the fund charges. *Forward P/E* = share price divided by next year's expected earnings — higher means you pay more per dollar of profit. *CAPE* = the same idea but averaged over 10 years, used to judge whether the market is expensive. *Drawdown* = how far the portfolio falls from its high. *Rebalancing* = selling a little of what has grown and buying what has shrunk, to get back to your target weights. *Withholding tax* = tax taken out of dividends before they reach you. *Bid-ask spread* = the small gap between the buying and selling price. *UCITS* = an EU-regulated fund wrapper (e.g. Irish-domiciled) that is outside US estate tax. *W-8BEN* = the form that tells a US broker you are a non-US person. *MPF/ORSO* = Hong Kong pension schemes, which cannot hold US ETFs.

You are 25. You have HKD 1 million and at least ten years. That combination is the single biggest advantage you have: **time**. Historically the US stock market has returned roughly **10% a year** over the long run (≈10.4% p.a. for 1996–2025 and ≈10.2% p.a. over about a century — both from secondary summaries, not a primary dataset), but it has also fallen **37% in a single year** (2008) and taken **5.5–7 years** to get back to its old high after the two worst crashes. So the plan is not "pick winners". The plan is:

1. **Own the whole US market cheaply** — one broad index ETF at 0.03% a year does the heavy lifting.
2. **Add a measured growth tilt** (Nasdaq-100) because you can tolerate volatility and have decades to recover.
3. **Keep 15% in cash-like and bond ETFs** as *dry powder* — money you can put into a crash rather than panic about. Note: this is not a hedge; the bond part can also fall (BND fell ~13% in 2022).
4. **Invest in three instalments over ~6 months** rather than all at once. This is a behaviour decision, not a market call: the market is expensive on the long-run CAPE measure (~40–41x, secondary sources) but only moderate on forward P/E (17.7x, below its 5-year average), and the Nasdaq-100 is already in a >10% correction — so there is no clear valuation signal either way, and staging simply spreads the entry risk.
5. **Rebalance once a year, and do nothing else.** The main risk to this plan is you, not the market.

**Proposed allocation (HKD 1,000,000):**

|---|---|---|---|---|
| US large-cap core | **VOO** (Vanguard S&P 500) | 50% | 500,000 | 63,735 |
| US growth tilt | **QQQ** (Invesco Nasdaq-100) | 20% | 200,000 | 25,494 |
| Non-US diversification | **VXUS** (Vanguard Total Intl ex-US) | 15% | 150,000 | 19,120 |

Equity 85% — of which **70% is US-listed US equity** (VOO + QQQ), **15% non-US equity** (VXUS) and **15% defensive** — inside your 10–20% defensive band. The 15% VXUS is a deliberate departure from a pure-US brief; if you want pure US, move it into VOO.

## 2. Why this allocation (the reasoning)

### 2.1 Why 85% equities at 25
- **Time is the edge.** Over 30 years the S&P 500 has compounded at roughly 10% a year (secondary summaries), and the index lost money in only 26 of 98 years from 1928 to 2025 (Damodaran data via VistaShares). A 25-year-old with a 10+ year horizon can let compounding work.
- **The drawdown is bigger than the 30–40% you assumed you could take.** Rebuilding a 2008-type year from the fact sheets, an 85/15 mix loses about **−33% in a calendar year** (≈HKD 670,000 left) but about **−45% to −50% peak-to-trough** in 2000–02 and 2008–09 (≈HKD 500,000–550,000 left). **This exceeds your stated tolerance.** Either accept that in writing, or cut QQQ to 10% and raise SGOV to 17% (which brings the peak-to-trough estimate to roughly −40%).
- **No income need** means we do not need dividend-heavy or bond-heavy holdings; total return is what matters.

### 2.2 Why VOO 50% is the core
- 0.03% expense ratio, ~USD 1.0 trillion in assets (order of magnitude; varies by source), 10-year annualised return **14.47%**, 5-year **10.33%** — the cheapest, most liquid way to own the 500 largest US companies.
- It is the "default" holding: if every other idea fails, this still delivers the market's return.

### 2.3 Why QQQ 20% (and not more)
- The Nasdaq-100 has returned **19.41% a year over 10 years** vs 14.47% for the S&P 500 — the growth/technology tilt has paid.
- But it is a tilt, not a core: in 2022 QQQ fell **−32.6%** while VOO fell 18.2%. At 20% weight, that difference costs about 3 percentage points of portfolio return in a bad year — acceptable; at 40% it would not be.
- Valuation support: the Nasdaq-100 forward P/E has compressed to **~18.7–19.2x** (secondary sources, varies by provider — unverified against FactSet) even as earnings estimates rose, and the index entered a >10% correction in November 2026. You are buying the growth sleeve after a de-rating, not at the top of one.
- The 20% cap is what keeps the tilt survivable: QQQ fell ~83% peak-to-trough in 2000–02.

### 2.4 Why VXUS 15% (non-US)
- The request is US stocks, so this is deliberately a minority sleeve: it is the only part of the portfolio that is not US-listed US equity, and it protects against a long stretch in which the US underperforms (historically non-US markets have had such stretches; no specific 2000–09 comparison was verified for this note).
- Cost 0.05%, 10-year return 8.82% — lower than the US, which is exactly why it is a diversifier and not the core.
- If you want a pure-US portfolio, move this 15% into VOO; the plan still works, it is just more concentrated.

### 2.5 Why 15% defensive (BND 8% + SGOV 7%)
- **Dry powder.** In a −30% year, this sleeve is what lets you rebalance *into* equities instead of selling.
- **SGOV yields ~3.3–3.4%** with almost no price risk (0–3 month T-bills) — roughly a zero *real* return while you wait.
- **BND yields ~4.2%** but has been a poor performer (5-year annualised **−0.24%**; it fell ~13% in 2022) because of the rate shock. We keep it small and treat its ~4.2% yield, not its history, as the forward guide. **BND is duration risk, not a hedge.**
- With the Fed at **3.75–4.00%** and press reports of the 18 Nov minutes saying most officials expect further hikes (secondary source), and the 10-year Treasury at **~5.05%**, cash and short bonds are genuinely competitive for the first time in years — that is the reason for holding 15% rather than 5%.

### 2.6 Why three instalments, not one
- The S&P 500's forward P/E is **17.7x** (below its 5-year average of 19.8x) but the **Shiller CAPE is ~40–41x** (secondary sources; verify against multpl.com/FactSet), the second-highest in ~150 years. Forward EPS estimates rose **+33.6%** year-to-date to 5 Nov 2026 while the index *price* rose only **+7.4%** — the market is cheaper *because earnings rose*, which is healthy, but a lot of good news is priced in.
- Splitting HKD 1,000,000 into **three tranches (40% / 30% / 30%) at roughly two-month intervals** reduces the risk of investing everything the week before a correction. It does not eliminate timing risk (lump-sum investing often wins on average — no source verified for this note), so we do not stretch it beyond ~6 months.

## 3. Implementation plan (simulated)

| Step | Timing | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Now | Open a US brokerage account (or a HK broker with US market access). Fund HKD 1,000,000; convert to USD in 2–3 tranches to smooth FX (the HKD peg limits this to ~1.2% either way). |
| 2 | Week 1 | Buy **40%** of each target weight: VOO 200,000 · QQQ 80,000 · VXUS 60,000 · BND 32,000 · SGOV 28,000 |
| 3 | ~Month 2 | Buy **30%** of each target weight |
| 4 | ~Month 4–6 | Buy the final **30%** |
| 5 | Annually | Rebalance back to target weights (sell what is over, buy what is under). Review the plan only if your life changes. |
| 6 | Ongoing | Reinvest dividends. Add new savings to whichever sleeve is furthest below target. |

**Costs to expect:** ETF expense ratios **~0.07% blended** (0.50×0.03 + 0.20×0.18 + 0.15×0.05 + 0.08×0.03 + 0.07×0.08 = 0.0665%); brokerage commission per trade (typically USD 0–5 at major brokers); bid-ask spreads are typically a few basis points for these large ETFs — check at order time. **US dividend withholding tax of 30%** applies to you as a Hong Kong resident (no US–HK tax treaty). Build-up: VOO 1.13%×30%×50% = 0.17% + QQQ 0.48%×30%×20% = 0.03% + VXUS 2.55%×30%×15% = 0.11% + BND 4.16%×30%×8% = 0.10% + SGOV 3.4%×30%×7% = 0.07% → **≈0.48% a year (≈HKD 4,800)**, a permanent drag that compounds. **All-in running cost ≈ 0.6–0.7% a year before trading costs.** US capital gains are not taxed for non-residents, and Hong Kong does not tax capital gains.

## 4. The risks — plainly

**1. You can lose a third of your money in a year — and half at the bottom.** The S&P 500 fell **−37.0%** in 2008 and **−22.1%** in 2002. On HKD 1,000,000, an 85/15 portfolio in a 2008-type *calendar year* would have been worth about **HKD 670,000**; at the 2008–09 *bottom* about **HKD 520,000**; at the 2000–02 bottom about **−48%, i.e. HKD 520,000–550,000**. That is the price of the long-run return.

**2. Recovery can take years, not months.** After the dot-com peak it took until **May 2007 (~7.2 years)** to regain the old high; after 2008, until **March 2013 (~5.5 years)**. COVID took 6 months; 2022 took about 2 years from the Jan-2022 peak (dates from secondary sources, not re-verified against S&P DJI). If you might need this money in under 5 years, this portfolio is wrong for you.

**3. Valuation risk.** CAPE ~40–41x (secondary sources) suggests future 10-year returns are likely below the ~10% historical average. **No quantified CAPE-implied return estimate is given here — none was verified, so do not rely on one.** The plan assumes lower returns than history, not higher.

**4. Concentration risk.** The largest US technology companies are a big share of both VOO and QQQ; the two sleeves overlap. A tech-led bear market hits both at once (2022: QQQ −32.6%, VOO −18.2%).

**5. Interest-rate risk.** The Fed is at 3.75–4.00% and the 10-year is at ~5.05%; the FOMC statement of 28 Oct 2026 held rates, and press reports of the 18 Nov minutes say most officials expect further hikes (secondary source). Higher rates pressure equity valuations and bond prices (BND fell ~13% in 2022). Cash yields help, but the "safe" sleeve is not risk-free in real terms.

**6. Currency risk.** Your spending is in HKD; your assets are in USD. The peg band (7.75–7.85) limits the swing to about **±0.6% around the mid-point (≈ ±HKD 6,000 on HKD 1m)** — arithmetic from the band width in the fact sheet, not a live quote, but a peg change would be a large event that a retail investor cannot hedge. USD strength/weakness also changes your HKD returns.

**7. Tax and estate risk (specific to a Hong Kong investor).**
- **30% US dividend withholding** — unavoidable on US-listed ETFs; ≈0.48% a year here (build-up in §3).
- **US estate tax:** non-US persons get only a **USD 60,000** exemption on US-situs assets, with rates of 18–40% above it. Your ~USD 127,000 portfolio is already above the threshold: the excess is **USD 67,000**, and at the top 40% marginal rate the exposure is roughly **USD 15,000–25,000** (graduated brackets not computed exactly); at USD 500,000 it would be ~USD 150,000–170,000. Mitigations to discuss with a tax adviser: Irish-domiciled UCITS equivalents (e.g. CSPX/VWRA — 15% fund-level withholding, outside US estate tax), joint accounts, or life insurance. This is a real, often-overlooked cost of "buying US stocks" directly.
- **Operational risk:** US market hours mean overnight order placement from Hong Kong; you must keep a **W-8BEN** form current (renewed every 3 years) or withholding rises; US ETFs cannot be held inside MPF/ORSO schemes; broker/custody risk applies.

**8. Behavioural risk — the biggest one.** You assumed you could take a 30–40% fall; this portfolio can fall **~45–50% peak-to-trough**. If that would make you sell, the equity weight must come down now. Rule: no selling in a drawdown; rebalance only on the annual date or when a sleeve drifts more than 5 percentage points.
