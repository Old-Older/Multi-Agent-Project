# AI on HKUST Campuses: The Rules Are Set Course by Course — and From 2026-27, Every Undergraduate Must Study AI

*For students and faculty, HKUST. Prepared for: university news site (main article), student social/WeChat post, campus bulletin script.*

---

## 1. MAIN ARTICLE (university news site) — 700–900 words

### The short version
- **What happened:** HKUST has kept its course-by-course AI rules and is adding a university-wide AI literacy requirement for undergraduates from 2026-27, backed by free AI tools for all staff and students.
- HKUST has **no single university-wide rule** on using generative AI in coursework. Since **Fall 2023-24**, each instructor sets the policy for their own course and must tell students in writing.
- From **2026-27**, every undergraduate admitted from that year must complete **six credits of AI and data literacy** (the HAIC requirement), with learning outcomes approved by the University Senate.
- Staff and students get free access to a one-stop **HKUST GenAI Platform** that now includes Hong Kong's homegrown **HKGAI V1** model — HKUST was the first local university to trial it (22 April 2025).
- In **November 2025** the university launched an **AI Literacy Hub** with Solomon Learning Group, tied to the Chief Executive's 2025 Policy Address digital-education push.
- **What it means for you:** check your course outline before you use AI; be ready to explain your work and show drafts; and if you are an instructor, your written course policy is now the operative rule.

### The story

**The lead:** HKUST has no single campus-wide rule on AI in coursework — each instructor decides, and from 2026-27 every new undergraduate must also study AI. Here is what that means for you.

Ask two HKUST students whether AI is allowed in their coursework and you may get two different answers — and both can be right. Under the university's **Policy for GenAI Integration in Teaching and Learning**, run by the Center for Education Innovation (CEI) and in force for courses from the 2023-24 academic year, the decision sits with the instructor:

> "Given the evolving landscape of GenAI tools, HKUST allows faculty members the flexibility to set their own course-level policies. Faculty members are required to communicate the chosen policy option to students in writing for courses offered in Fall 2023-24."

The policy, CEI says, was "formulated in consultation with faculty members and students from both undergraduate and postgraduate courses," with the aim of providing "a student-friendly, adaptable framework in light of the rapidly evolving AI technologies."

That design is deliberate. A single campus-wide ban or a single campus-wide permission would be out of date within a semester; a course-by-course rule lets a programming instructor require AI-assisted coding while a seminar in ethics restricts it. The cost is that the rule a student actually lives under is the one printed in their course outline — which is why CEI tells students to "consult your instructor for guidance," and suggests that, whether or not AI tools are used, students "should be prepared to explain their work, justify their sources, provide drafts, and discuss alternative perspectives."

For instructors, that shifts the work from policing to design. CEI's guidance is that detection tools alone are not a reliable answer to AI-generated work, and it publishes assessment-design material for staff who want to set tasks that hold up when students have a chatbot open. The university has also run professional-development courses on generative AI for teaching staff, including a micro-credential series (GENAI-001 and GENAI-004) through CEI's Academy of Education Excellence.

### From permission to requirement

The bigger change is coming for students who have not yet arrived. From **2026-27**, every undergraduate admitted from that year must complete **six dedicated credits of AI and data literacy** — the **HAIC** (Human-AI Co-Creation and Data Literacy) requirement, listed as a new Common Core area. The Provost's Office describes it as "six dedicated credits of AI and data literacy for every undergraduate, beginning in 2026–27," and says the commitment "makes HKUST one of the first universities in the region to guarantee that all students, regardless of major, are able to use AI thoughtfully, work alongside intelligent systems, and judge AI's impact on society."

The three learning outcomes — understand AI and data; collaborate with AI; evaluate AI in society — were approved by the University Senate. In practice that means a history major and a computer-science major will share a common floor of AI knowledge: what models are, where they fail, how to work with them, and what they do to privacy, bias and power.

### The tools on the desk

None of this is abstract. On **22 April 2025**, HKUST became the first Hong Kong university to give staff and students free access to **HKGAI V1**, the territory's first homegrown large language model, developed by the Hong Kong Generative AI Research and Development Center — a joint-university venture led by HKUST. It sits on the university's one-stop **HKUST GenAI Platform** alongside other models, and supports Cantonese, Putonghua and English. Provost Prof. Guo Yike said the addition would "better meet the needs of our faculty and students as they explore innovative pedagogical approaches and engage in collaborative research." The university said it would provide training support for faculty.

Then, in **November 2025**, HKUST announced an **AI Literacy Hub** in strategic collaboration with Solomon Learning Group, framed as a major AI-education initiative responding to the digital-education measures in the Chief Executive's 2025 Policy Address. The Hub is directed by Dr Jac Leung.

### Why it matters

**Why it matters:** the rule you are judged by is the one in your own course outline, and from 2026-27 AI literacy becomes part of every HKUST degree.

Hong Kong's universities are autonomous on this question — the Government has said so in LegCo — which means there is no territory-wide rule to fall back on, and each institution's choices set the terms for its own students. HKUST's answer has been to decentralise the decision to the instructor and then, from 2026-27, to guarantee a common baseline of AI literacy for everyone. For students, the practical advice is unglamorous: read the course outline, ask before you use a tool, and keep your drafts. For faculty, the written policy in your syllabus is no longer paperwork — it is the rule your students will be judged by.
