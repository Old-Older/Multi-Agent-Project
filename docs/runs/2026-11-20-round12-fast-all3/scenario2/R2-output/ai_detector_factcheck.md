# Can AI-writing detectors reliably tell whether a student wrote an assignment?

**A fact-check for students and faculty**

**The claim:** *"AI-writing detectors can reliably determine whether a university student's assignment was written by AI."*

**Verdict: Not supported as stated.** No study we examined shows that any available detector reliably determines whether a student's assignment was written by AI. Detectors can sometimes spot unedited machine text, but independent testing finds high error rates, errors that fall disproportionately on non-native English writers, and easy evasion by paraphrasing or light editing. The leading commercial vendor and a national regulator both say detector scores should not be relied on as proof. What remains genuinely uncertain is how far better-designed processes — multiple tools, human review, and clear rules — could reduce the harm; no study we examined shows they make a single score reliable.

*How we tested it: we compared the claim with peer-reviewed testing, the technical literature on detection and evasion, vendor documentation, regulator guidance and documented misconduct cases. Our researchers could not open every primary paper in full, so figures rest on abstracts, publisher pages and search snippets; see the note under Sources.*

## What "reliably" would have to mean

A detector is reliable for this purpose only if it is right in the cases that matter: real student assignments, including ones a student drafted and then edited, and ones written by students working in a second language. It would need a low false-positive rate (human work wrongly flagged) *and* a low false-negative rate (AI work that slips through), and it would need to hold up when a student has an incentive to evade it. Measured against that standard, the evidence fails on every count.

## The evidence we examined

**1. Independent testing finds no tool that is accurate overall.** Weber-Wulff and colleagues (*International Journal for Educational Integrity*, 2023) tested 14 tools — including Turnitin, GPTZero and OpenAI's classifier — on 54 controlled documents across six categories. According to the study's published findings, no tool reached 80% overall accuracy. The category closest to real student practice, AI text that a human then edited, scored around 42% of the maximum, against about 74% for unedited AI text; the authors concluded the tools are "neither accurate nor reliable." Machine-paraphrased AI text largely escaped detection — the case the authors found most striking.
**2. False positives are systematic, and they fall on a defined group.** Liang and colleagues, in *Patterns* (July 2023), ran 91 human-written TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers and 88 essays by US eighth-graders through seven widely used detectors. The detectors were near-perfect on the eighth-grade essays but produced an average false-positive rate of about 61% on the human-written TOEFL essays; all seven unanimously misclassified about 20% of them as AI-generated. The cause is structural: detectors look for predictable, low-variation prose, and a careful second-language writer's textbook English looks predictable. A tool can be "accurate on average" and still be wrong most of the time for a specific group.
**3. The best-resourced developer retired its own detector.** OpenAI's AI classifier, launched in January 2023, identified only 26% of AI-written text as likely AI-written while flagging 9% of human-written text as AI, on OpenAI's own challenge set. OpenAI retired it on 20 July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy."

**4. Evasion is cheap.** Krishna and colleagues (arXiv, 2023; NeurIPS 2023) showed that a paraphraser called DIPPER cut DetectGPT's detection accuracy from 70.3% to 4.6% at a 1% false-positive rate without changing meaning; ordinary "rewrite this in your own words" prompting is the same attack in weaker form. Sadasivan and colleagues (arXiv, revised January 2025) show a recursive-paraphrasing attack breaking watermarking and neural detectors, and argue that as models imitate human text better, detection performance degrades toward random guessing. Watermarking — the most defensible approach in principle, and the subject of Google DeepMind's SynthID-Text paper in *Nature* (October 2024) — only identifies text from a watermarked generator, and a 2025 assessment found it vulnerable to paraphrasing, copy-paste and back-translation.**5. Detectors cannot tell "AI-assisted" from "AI-generated."** No detector we examined measures how much of a text was AI-edited, or distinguishes "AI drafted, student revised" from "student drafted, AI polished." Turnitin's AI report is documented, in a university teaching-centre guide, as carrying the warning "Percentage may not indicate academic misconduct. Review required."**6. The vendor and a regulator say the score is not proof.** Turnitin's help documentation (undated) states that its model "may not always be accurate (it may misidentify both human and AI-generated text) so it should not be used as the sole basis for adverse actions against a student." Australia's tertiary regulator TEQSA, in *The evolving risk to academic integrity posed by generative artificial intelligence* (August 2024), advises institutions to "limit reliance on AI detectors," noting that testing "continually demonstrates that they are unreliable and tend to produce false positive results."**7. Real cases show the cost of getting it wrong.** In May 2023 a Texas A&M-Commerce professor told his class that ChatGPT itself had "taken credit" for their assignments and threatened to fail the class and withhold diplomas; the university intervened. In June 2023 a UC Davis graduating senior was flagged by Turnitin for work she had written herself and had to defend herself in a misconduct process. The UK's Quality Assurance Agency reports students describing "anxiety around possible accusations" and a "reluctance to declare even legitimate GenAI use for fear of how it would be received."### The counter-evidence, for fairness

Detectors are not useless in every setting. A 2025 study in *Open Information Science* found very few false positives when Turnitin was run on human-written introduction sections published in 2016, and reported that earlier research has found Turnitin's low-false-positive claims to be broadly accurate, at the cost of a somewhat higher false-negative rate. Turnitin's then chief product officer, Annie Chechitelli, has claimed a document-level false-positive rate under 1% for documents with more than 20% AI content, validated against 800,000 pre-GPT samples (reported by THE Journal, May 2023). That is a vendor self-assessment on a vendor-designed sample, scoped to documents that are mostly AI; it does not cover the borderline, mixed-authorship work that actually generates disputes, and it sits awkwardly beside the 61% figure from independent testing on real non-native essays. Both numbers can be true of their own test sets — which is exactly why no single number supports "reliably."
## What remains uncertain

- **Whether human review plus multiple tools can salvage "reliably".** This is the strongest version of the claim, and the evidence is mixed. Some researchers argue detection is feasible in principle if you have many samples from the same generator (Chakraborty et al., ICML 2024 position paper) — a condition a single student essay does not meet. Aggregating several detectors and requiring human review may reduce false positives (Arizona State University, *Advances in Physiology Education*, 2025), but that is a proposed fix, not evidence that current practice is reliable, and no study we examined measured a combined process on real assignments. The vendor's own warning and TEQSA's advice both assume human review is already in place — and both still say the score is not proof.
- **Whether disclosure and process rules matter more than detection.** On the evidence they carry more weight than any score: a detector cannot tell permitted assistance from misconduct, so what decides a case is what the institution's rules require students to declare and what process it gives them. The QAA's finding that students hide even legitimate AI use suggests the rules, not the tools, are where the leverage is — but we found no study comparing the two approaches, so this is an inference, not a measured result.
- **How widely detectors are used in misconduct cases.** We could not verify a credible figure for how many faculty use detectors in misconduct cases, and we have not published one.
- **The size of the problem in any single institution.** Error rates vary by tool, threshold, text type and student population; a university's own false-positive rate is an empirical question it has usually not measured.- **The literature is a dispute, not a settled consensus.** Sadasivan et al. argue reliable detection is unachievable against an adversary; Chakraborty et al. argue it is achievable under conditions. Both agree the single-document, real-world academic case is the hard one.
## What this means for faculty

- **Never use a detector score as sole evidence in a misconduct case.** The vendor says it should not be the sole basis for adverse action; a national regulator says detectors are unreliable. Treat a score as a reason to look, never as a finding.
- If a score raises a concern, investigate the work — drafts, version history, a conversation about the argument — not the percentage. If you record a score, record it as a screening flag.
- Be explicit that non-native English writers are at higher risk of being wrongly flagged, and weigh that before escalating.
- Prefer assessment designs that make provenance visible (in-class writing, oral defence, staged drafts) over detection, and make your disclosure rules explicit — clearer rules do more work than any detector.
## What this means for students

- A flag is not a finding. Ask what evidence beyond the score exists, and ask for the process your institution's policy provides — including your right to respond and to appeal. A score alone should not decide a misconduct case.
- Keep drafts and version history; they are the most useful thing you can produce if questioned.
- Being flagged does not mean you did anything wrong — the published error rates show honest work is flagged, especially second-language writing.
- If your institution permits AI assistance, declare it as your policy requires; concealment, not disclosure, is what turns a permitted use into a misconduct case.
